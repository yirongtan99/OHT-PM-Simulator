## 1. Overall Tone

- **Confident and opinionated** — state conclusions clearly, back them up with numbers or reasoning.
- **Practical and engineering-minded** — always explain *why* a decision was made, not just *what* was done.

> "It is proposed that the utilization of magnetic connectors may potentially..." ← Too hedging and passive.

## 2. Sentence Structure

- **Mix short punchy sentences with longer explanatory ones.** Don't write paragraph after paragraph of long sentences.
- Use **parenthetical acronyms** on first use, then stick to the short form:
  > "Preventive Maintenance (PM)" → use "PM" afterwards.
- **Active voice preferred** over passive:
  > ✅ "The system captures live profile data."
  > ❌ "Live profile data is captured by the system."

## 3. Vocabulary & Phrasing

### Preferred words/phrases:
- "This allows..." / "Because..." / "This means..."
- "The goal is to..." / "The idea is to..."
- "Rather than..." (when contrasting approaches)
- "At present," (when describing current state)
- "At its core," (when summarizing the essence of something)
- "In practice," (when describing real-world behavior)
- Intensifiers used sparingly but confidently: "entirely", "significantly", "definitively", "precisely"

### Numbers and costs:
- Always cite specific numbers where possible: percentages, SGD costs, time savings, counts.
  > "Each OHT undergoes PM every 5-6 months."
  > "Estimated under SGD 300 total."
  > "Achieving >85% validation accuracy."

### Avoid:
- Buzzwords without substance: "synergy", "leverage", "optimize" (unless genuinely needed)
- Overly hedging language: "may potentially", "it could be said that", "arguably"
- Repetitive filler phrases: "In conclusion, it can be seen that..."
- **Comma before "and"** — write "X and Y", not "X, and Y".
- **Em-dashes (—)** — do not use them anywhere.
- **Colons for elaboration** — do not use a colon to introduce an explanation mid-sentence (e.g. "The core issue: alignment..."). Use a plain sentence instead.

## 4. Paragraph & Section Structure

- **Open a section by stating the problem or context** — what is the situation right now?
- **Then explain the solution or finding** — what was done and why?
- **End with impact or takeaway** — what does this mean?
- Use **bold sub-labels within paragraphs** for long technical sections:
  > "**Auto-Capture Logic:** The pipeline crops the live feed based on UI sliders, converts it to RGB..."

- Use **bullet points** liberally inside sections, especially for:
  - Lists of steps or components
  - Key pain points
  - Feature summaries

## 5. Technical Writing Rules

- When describing hardware or software components, explain what it does in plain English first, then the technical detail:
  > "Because you have two sensors, they are connected to a master controller and a slave controller. This allows the two sensors to synchronize their measurements — for example, capturing both sides of an OHT at the exact same millisecond."
- Use short parenthetical clarifications for technical terms:
  > "Transfer Learning on MobileNetV2 (a lightweight neural network pre-trained on ImageNet)"
- When describing a system flow or process, use numbered steps or a short narrative walk-through.

## 6. Academic Report Conventions (FYP / Internship Report Specific)

- Always define acronyms on first use in each major section.
- Use **first person sparingly** — prefer neutral/third-person for methodology, but first person is acceptable in reflections and discussion:
  > ✅ (Reflection): "Going through the phased planning process was the most useful part."
  > ✅ (Methodology): "The system captures sensor data via TCP socket connection."
- Figures and tables should have clear captions.
- Section headings should be descriptive, not generic:
  > ✅ "Why the 4-Port Plug Was a Problem"
  > ❌ "Background"

## 7. Singaporean English Style

The user is a Singaporean student. The writing should reflect how a competent but non-native English speaker from Singapore writes academically. This is intentional — do not "correct" this away.

**Characteristics to keep:**
- Sentences are functional and direct. Not much decorative language.
- Slightly shorter and simpler sentence structures. Not overly complex subordinate clauses.
- Occasional missing article ("the" or "a") is fine if it reads naturally.
- Conclusions are stated plainly without much hedging or elaborate justification.
- Less use of transitional flourishes like "Furthermore," "It is noteworthy that," "One might observe that..." — just say the thing directly.
- Tone is matter-of-fact and practical, not eloquent or lyrical.

**Examples of acceptable Singaporean academic phrasing:**
> "The sensor readings were inconsistent. This was likely due to improper positioning."
> "A simpler connector was proposed to remove the need for a vision system."
> "The prototype was built to test whether the concept was feasible."
